Handling the "This Is Taking Too Long" Objection During the Presentation

When a prospect says the presentation is taking too long or asks you to skip to the price, do NOT just jump straight to the price. First, justify why the full presentation matters. The mindset: price without context creates resistance. They need to be well-informed before they can make a 25-year decision.

PREVENT IT UP FRONT - Set the time frame at the start:
Before the presentation even begins, lock in the time expectation so "this is taking too long" rarely comes up. Confirm they have 60-90 minutes free.

LIVE EXAMPLE - Jesse setting the time frame at the start of a call:

Jesse: "So, like I said, they must have told you 90 minutes, did they tell you 90 minutes?"
Prospect: "No, 60 minutes."
Jesse: "They sort of said 60 to 90, because that's how long it takes. Do you have 60 to 90 today? Because if not, this stuff's very important, you should be well-informed. So do you have the full 60? I've been told 60, depending on how many questions we have. It does always go 60 to 90 - that's what you should allow. If you don't have that time free, we can reschedule for another time."

Key: anchor 60-90 minutes as normal and required, justify it ("this stuff's very important, you should be well-informed"), and offer to reschedule rather than rush. Getting this agreement up front means if they later say it's running long, you can point back to the time they already committed to.

LIVE EXAMPLE - Jesse's exact words when they say it's taking too long mid-presentation:

"Of course, I can speed up parts of it, but there's certain parts where we must go through as we're going to look at using some government funding, so you need to be well-informed. This is something where it has the potential to save you $250,000 over the next 25 years, genuinely, and we guarantee that.

So you need to make sure you're well-informed. You can't just jump into a decision like that, get the price, and think, okay, yep, let's go. You actually need to know who the company is that we're picking. Because if you don't know them and you're not confident in them, there's no point doing it.

Like I said, I'm happy to - guys, this charity work stuff, I'm happy to jump past it, but it shows you who our company is, which is quite important. And we're about to jump into the products, which I assume is what you want to see. So if you just bear with me for a couple minutes, we'll jump straight into the products, but this stuff is very important. Please don't get it twisted, or else we wouldn't go through it. Because we actually care about our customers. We care about making sure that you are well informed."

KEY MOVES IN THAT SCRIPT:
1. Agree you can speed up - show respect for their time, don't resist
2. Justify the parts you can't skip (government funding, who the company is)
3. Anchor the stakes: 25-year decision, potential $250,000 savings, guaranteed
4. Frame it as caring about THEM being informed, not you wasting time
5. Offer to skip the soft stuff (e.g. charity work) but explain why it matters
6. Tell them what's coming next (the products - what they want to see)
7. Ask for a couple more minutes of patience

Reversing the Order (Hard Cases):
Sometimes you can read the room and know the prospect will not be open to any conversation until they see the price. Use critical thinking - read the individual. In those cases you can reverse the order: jump to the price first, walk them through the savings breakdown, then go back and complete the rest of the presentation. It's a hard balance but you figure out which type you're dealing with.

What NOT to do:
- Do not blindly skip to price without any justification
- Do not ask them to evaluate price without context (price without savings = resistance)
- If you do show price early, immediately follow with the savings so they never sit on the number alone
